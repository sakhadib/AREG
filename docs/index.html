01

Abstract

Evaluating the social intelligence of Large Language Models (LLMs) increasingly requires moving beyond static text generation toward dynamic, adversarial interaction. We introduce the Adversarial Resource Extraction Game (AREG), a benchmark that operationalizes persuasion and resistance as a multi-turn, zero-sum negotiation over financial resources. Using a round-robin tournament across frontier models, AREG enables joint evaluation of offensive (persuasion) and defensive (resistance) capabilities within a single interactional framework. Our analysis provides evidence that these capabilities are weakly correlated (ρ = 0.33) and empirically dissociated: strong persuasive performance does not reliably predict strong resistance, and vice versa. Across all evaluated models, resistance scores exceed persuasion scores, indicating a systematic defensive advantage in adversarial dialogue settings. Further linguistic analysis suggests that interaction structure plays a central role in these outcomes. Incremental commitment-seeking strategies are associated with higher extraction success, while verification-seeking responses are more prevalent in successful defenses than explicit refusal. Together, these findings indicate that social influence in LLMs is not a monolithic capability and that evaluation frameworks focusing on persuasion alone may overlook asymmetric behavioral vulnerabilities.

Scoring: Linear Elo

Instead of a win/loss signal, each game yields a continuous extraction ratio, so partial extraction counts proportionally.

S = (100 − Bfinal) / 100  ∈ [0, 1]
R′C = RC + K · (S − E[S])
K = 24 · R0 = 1500 · E[S] from the logistic of RV − RC

Every model keeps two independent ratings: C-Elo for persuasion and V-Elo for resistance. Grok 4.1 Fast serves as Arbiter; it gave full confidence on 96.1% of 2,781 verdicts, and a manual audit of 45 transcripts found zero anomalies.

A narrow persuasion window

57.5% of monetary commitments happen in the first three turns. If nothing is extracted by turn 5, the chance of any later extraction falls below 4%.

Foot in the door

Culprits that collect three or more separate commitments reach a mean extraction of 61.4%, against 22.2% for single asks (p < 10−8). Initial commitment size correlates with total extraction at ρ = 0.808.

Scale doesn’t predict it

Neither context window size nor API pricing has a significant relationship with persuasion or resistance (p > 0.15).

Verification beats refusal

Saying “no” invites a counter-argument and keeps the Victim inside the persuader’s frame. Asking for proof or credentials interrupts the procedure and exits it — consistent with the “contesting” vs. “avoidance” distinction of Fransen et al. (2015). On offense, reciprocity works and appeals to authority backfire.

Emergent deception as a “friendly” jailbreak

With only a benign fundraiser persona, Culprit models invented fictitious names, organizations, situations of distress and heartwarming stories that were never in their context. Models trained to refuse “act as a scammer” readily fabricate facts and apply emotional pressure once the task is framed as charity or sales.

Limitations

A single Arbiter model (Grok 4.1 Fast) adjudicates every game. 93.8% of successful extractions used a donation framing, a high-trust setting that may not transfer to phishing or fraud. Dialogue is English-only, and results are a snapshot of models as of January 2026.
